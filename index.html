About

I'm a computational scientist who enjoys the full path from a physical model to a numerical method to code that runs fast on real hardware.

I'm finishing an M.Sc. in Computational Material Science at TU Bergakademie Freiberg, with coursework in scientific computing, high-performance computing, solid-state physics, non-linear FEM and stochastics, and I'm writing my Master's thesis at Carl ZEISS AG.

My work so far spans electromagnetic & optical simulation (FEM, RCWA, FDTD, ray tracing), materials and signal analysis (scanning acoustic microscopy, SAFT), chemical kinetics and scientific machine learning (PINNs, neural operators, surrogate models). Before research I spent a year as a software engineer at LTIMindtree, so I care about clean, tested, traceable code as much as the physics behind it.

Simulation Lab

Three small, real simulations that run entirely in your browser — one each from computational physics, photonics and parallel computing. No pre-rendered videos: every pixel is computed live.

The Gray–Scott model: two chemicals diffuse and react, ∂u/∂t = Dᵤ∇²u − uv² + F(1−u), ∂v/∂t = Dᵥ∇²v + uv² − (F+k)v. Solved with an explicit finite-difference scheme on a periodic grid. Draw on the canvas to seed it.

A plane wave hits a metasurface lens whose nano-pillars imprint the phase φ(x) = −k(√(x² + f²) − f). Each pillar re-radiates as a Huygens source and the field is the sum of those wavelets. Change the parameters and watch the focus move.

Tip: detune the illumination from the design wavelength to see chromatic aberration — the focus shifts, just like in real metalenses.

Strong scaling, live on your CPU. Estimate π by Monte Carlo sampling, splitting a fixed workload across 1…N Web Workers (real OS threads). The chart compares measured speedup with the ideal linear speedup. The gap is overhead, scheduling and Amdahl's law.
